I build robots that see and fly on their own. My work spans autonomous drones, digital twins and on-device AI: GPS-denied flight with PX4 and visual-inertial odometry, simulation-to-reality pipelines in NVIDIA Isaac Sim, and real-time perception that runs entirely on embedded hardware such as NVIDIA Jetson.

I’m with the Biomimetics and Intelligent Systems Group (BISG) at the University of Oulu, Finland. Current threads:

  • Digital twins for drone fleets. bisg_isaac runs simulated and real PX4/ZED drones through the same ROS 2 containers and code.
  • Active 3D capture. Kinetix plans next-best views so a monocular drone can reconstruct objects and whole rooms in fewer flights.
  • Perception datasets and benchmarks. LiDAR range-image segmentation of roadside infrastructure (PoleSight) and UAV scene understanding.
  • Autonomy on the edge. Onboard YOLO and DeepStream pipelines, from search-and-detect drones (RAPTOR, UltraHack 2026) to assistive vision for visually impaired users.

  1. IVCNZ
    RangeGen PoleSight: A Benchmark Dataset and Scalable Range-Image Generation Framework for Roadside Pole Detection
    Timo Mäenpää*, Akash Bappy*, Siva Ariram, Durga Prasad Bavirisetti, and Antti Tikanmäki
    In International Conference on Image and Vision Computing New Zealand (IVCNZ). *Equal contribution , 2026
    Abs Bib

    Roadside pole-like infrastructure supports asset inventory, high-definition mapping, and autonomous-vehicle localization, yet Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) benchmarks with fine-grained pole categories remain scarce. This paper presents PoleSight, a range-image instance-segmentation dataset, and RangeGen, a scalable framework for generating annotated range views from large-scale mobile laser scanning point clouds. RangeGen projects dense point clouds from virtual sensor poses, transfers manually verified two-dimensional annotations back to their source three-dimensional points, and reprojects the labeled cloud to obtain additional annotated frames without repeated manual labeling. PoleSight provides 1,088 range images with return intensity and 6,121 polygon instances of light, fence, traffic, and gantry-sign poles. Fifteen segmentation baselines, five variants each from three YOLO families, reach a best box mean average precision (mAP) of 29.21 and a best mask value of 11.93 across standard COCO thresholds on a seen-location split, in which test frames usually show poles that are also present in training. That gap identifies precise mask recovery, rather than localization, as the open challenge on this benchmark.
